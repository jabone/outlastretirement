Which account do we draw from?

Brokerage, IRA/401(k) and Roth: the withdrawal order, the tax, and why a dividend isn’t extra money.

Big decisions · 9 pictures

This is an educational walkthrough using a fictional family. It is not financial advice, and the right answers for you depend on facts a lesson cannot know.

Watch this lesson Opens YouTube in a new tab.

1.Which account do we draw from?

Once the paychecks stop, the money you spend comes out of your savings. But savings aren’t one pot. They sit in different kinds of accounts, and each kind is taxed its own way. So which account do you draw from first? As always: this is an educational walkthrough using a fictional family. It isn’t financial advice.

2.Three kinds of accounts

There are three main kinds of accounts. A brokerage account is the plain investment account. Its dividends and interest are taxed every year, and when you sell, only the profit is taxed. Most dividends, and profit on investments held more than a year, are taxed at lower rates, sometimes zero. A traditional IRA or 401(k) is pre-tax: you skipped the tax when the money went in, so every dollar you take out is taxed as income. A Roth is the other way around: you paid the tax going in, so withdrawals after fifty-nine and a half, from a Roth at least five years old, aren’t taxed at all. There’s also a health savings account: money spent on medical costs isn’t taxed.

3.Where Ray and Angie’s savings are

Here are Ray and Angie. Today they have a hundred fifty-nine thousand dollars saved. A hundred fifty thousand is in their 401(k)s, and nine thousand is in a brokerage account. They have no Roth. By the year before Ray retires, the 401(k)s have grown to about three hundred thirty-one thousand dollars in today’s money, and the brokerage account to about fourteen thousand. So almost every dollar they take from savings will come out of a 401(k), and be taxed as income.

4.The withdrawal order

The app lets you choose the order. The standard order is the brokerage account first, then the IRA or 401(k), then the Roth. The idea is simple. Selling in a brokerage account is taxed only on the profit, and while you spend it, your 401(k) and Roth keep growing without yearly tax. The Roth goes last because its growth is never taxed and it has no required withdrawals. You can pick another order on the Plan page, under Spending, in Withdrawals and Taxable Account. None of them is right for everyone, and the app shows you what each one does.

5.Ray and Angie’s first years retired

Here is their plan on the Year by Year page, with Accounts selected. In 2037, Ray’s first year retired, the plan takes fourteen thousand four hundred dollars from the brokerage account, all of it, and nineteen thousand dollars from the 401(k)s. Social Security pays the rest. From 2038 on, every withdrawal comes from the 401(k)s: about thirty-four thousand dollars that year, while Angie still buys her own health insurance, then about twenty thousand a year once she turns sixty-five and is on Medicare, the government health plan.

6.Why they pay almost no tax

Here is the part that surprises people. Even though every 401(k) dollar is taxable, Ray and Angie pay almost no federal income tax in retirement: less than a hundred dollars in 2037, and nothing from 2039 through 2044. Their withdrawals are small. Everyone gets a standard deduction, an amount of income the tax law never taxes, and it is bigger after sixty-five. That covers most of their income. Only part of their Social Security counts, and at their income, profit from selling long-held investments is taxed at zero. In 2038, when they took out thirty-four thousand, the tax rose to about two thousand. Larger withdrawals would use up the deduction, make more of their Social Security taxable, and push the top dollars into higher brackets. They live in Texas, which has no state income tax; in most states the bill would be higher. So how much you take out each year matters as much as where it comes from.

7.A dividend isn’t extra money

You may have heard of living off dividends. A dividend isn’t extra money. When a fund pays one, its share price drops by about the same amount, so the account is worth about the same. Inside a 401(k), the dividend simply stays in the 401(k). Spending it is still a 401(k) withdrawal, taxed as income. In a brokerage account, a dividend is taxed in the year it is paid, whether you spend it or reinvest it. That is why the plan looks at how much you take from each account, not at dividends. So spending a dividend just means selling a little less.

8.Required withdrawals

One more rule. For anyone born in 1960 or later, the IRS requires a minimum withdrawal from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s every year from age seventy-five. Ray’s first comes in 2047: about sixty-six hundred dollars. They already take about twenty-three thousand a year, so nothing changes. If the required amount were ever more than you need, you still pay income tax on all of it. The plan puts the part you don’t spend into your brokerage account.

9.Your turn: see where your money comes from

Now it’s your turn. Open Ray and Angie’s plan, or your own, and go to Year by Year. Press Accounts to see which account pays for each year, and Taxes to see what each year costs. Then, on the Plan page under Spending, open Withdrawals and Taxable Account and try a different withdrawal order. Watch what happens to the taxes and to the verdict. Your plan stays private on your device. This is education, not financial advice.
